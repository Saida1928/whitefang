Origin

Born of Ice
and Stone

White Fang came into the world in the cold grey of a Canadian dawn — a world of frost and fang, of silence broken only by the wind. His story is not one of domestication but of transformation: a wild thing learning what it means to trust, and a man learning what it means to be trusted.

Jack London wrote him not as a pet, but as a mirror — a creature through which we examine our own impulses toward dominance, violence, and ultimately, love.

Chronicle

History of
the Wolf

From the ancient steppes to the boreal forests — the wolf's journey through time mirrors our own.

300,000 BC

The First Wolf

Canis lupus emerges from the Eurasian steppe — a social predator unlike anything before it. Built for endurance, designed for cooperation, the grey wolf becomes one of the most successful large carnivores in history.

15,000 BC

First Contact

Wolves begin shadowing human camps, drawn by warmth and scraps. Some grow bolder than others. A relationship unlike any between humans and wild animals begins to take shape — uncertain, dangerous, and transformative for both.

10,000 BC

Domestication

The genetic divergence that would produce the domestic dog is now underway. But the wild wolf continues unchanged — still hunting the tundra, still singing to the moon, still raising pups in earthen dens beside frozen rivers.

1600s

The Persecution

European settlers carry old fears to new continents. Systematic extermination campaigns begin across North America and Europe. Within three centuries, wolves are eradicated from most of their historical range — silent forests where howls once echoed.

1906

White Fang Published

Jack London gives the wolf a name, a voice, and an inner life that forces readers to reconsider what they feared. The novel becomes a turning point in how Western culture imagines the relationship between the wild and the human.

1995

The Return

Grey wolves are reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park after a 70-year absence. The ecological cascade that follows — the reshaping of rivers, the return of songbirds, the recovery of forests — becomes one of conservation's defining stories.

Taxonomy

Types of
the Wolf

Over 38 recognized subspecies inhabit the earth's wildest places — from arctic tundra to dense temperate forest. Each shaped by its landscape.

Arctic Wolf

Canis lupus arctos

Pure white coat evolved for arctic camouflage. Found across the Canadian Arctic Archipelago and northern Greenland. The only subspecies that lives year-round in the high Arctic — enduring months of polar darkness with a metabolism tuned to extremes.

Grey Wolf

Canis lupus lupus

The most widespread and largest of all wolves. Coat ranges from near-white to jet black, with every shade of grey and tawny in between. Once inhabited almost all of the Northern Hemisphere — forests, plains, deserts, and mountains alike.

Timber Wolf

Canis lupus occidentalis

The largest North American subspecies, built for the deep boreal forest and mountain ranges of the Rockies. It was this wolf — powerful, territorial, and fiercely social — that London drew on when he imagined White Fang's ancestors.

Ethiopian Wolf

Canis simensis

The rarest canid on earth. Flame-red and lean, it haunts the Afroalpine highlands of Ethiopia at elevations above 3,000 metres. Fewer than 500 survive. A reminder that the wolf story, for some, is still a story of disappearing.

Mexican Wolf

Canis lupus baileyi

The smallest and most endangered grey wolf in North America. Once extinct in the wild, a captive breeding programme has returned it to the mountains of Arizona and New Mexico — a fragile, hard-won comeback still in the making.

Indian Wolf

Canis lupus pallipes

Slender, tawny, and built for scrubland rather than snow. Found across India, Iran, and Israel. One of the oldest lineages, genetically distinct — a wolf shaped not by ice and pine but by heat, dust, and the arid edge of the world.

Pack Life

The Family
of Wolves

A wolf alone is powerful. A wolf pack is something close to remarkable — a society built on kinship, cooperation, and a hierarchy maintained not by cruelty but by trust.

The Alpha Pair

Pack Leaders

The breeding pair who lead the pack — not through aggression but through experience. They choose the territory, coordinate the hunt, and set the tone of the group. Their bond is for life. When one is lost, the other is never quite the same again.

The Beta Wolves

Senior Pack Members

Second in rank, first into the fray. The beta wolves form the muscle and memory of the pack — experienced hunters who pass skill to the next generation. They serve as intermediaries, enforcers of order, and the pack's most reliable problem-solvers.

The Pups

The Next Generation

Born in early spring, raised by the entire pack. Every adult takes a role — babysitter, teacher, food-bringer. The pup is not merely a future hunter; it is the reason the pack coheres, the centre of gravity around which everything orbits.

The Code

Laws of
the Pack

The rules that hold a wolf pack together across hundreds of miles of wilderness — older than any human institution.

01

The Hunt is Shared

No wolf eats alone if the pack made the kill. The hierarchy governs order of feeding, but every member from alpha to omega receives a share. This is not generosity — it is strategy. A pack that starves its own weakens itself.

02

Pups Are Protected by All

The entire pack treats the season's pups as shared responsibility. Aunts, uncles, older siblings — all participate in raising the young. It is one of the few truly cooperative childcare systems among non-human mammals.

03

Territory is Defended

A pack's range is marked with scent and howl — a living boundary that takes constant maintenance. Intrusion is met with coordinated response. But actual violence between packs is rarer than myth suggests; display often settles what combat would decide with blood.

04

The Sick Are Not Abandoned

Research has documented pack members carrying food to injured wolves, slowing travel for the old, and staying close to the dying. The pack is not merely a hunting unit — it is a community with something that looks very much like care.

05

Howl to Be Found

The wolf's howl is not threat or display — it is communication across impossible distances. A pack separated during a hunt reconvenes through song. In the silence of the boreal winter, sound carries further than sight, and the voice becomes the bond.

The Dark Season

When the Sun
Does Not Rise

The arctic winter is not metaphorical — it is 67 days of continuous night. Temperatures drop to −50°C. Prey grows scarce. In that darkness, the pack pulls closer, moving as a single organism, sharing warmth against a cold that has killed everything that did not adapt to it over hundreds of thousands of years.

Legacy

The Wolf
Endures

More than a century after London wrote him, White Fang remains the defining portrait of the wild animal at the threshold of civilisation — not broken by human contact, but shaped by it in ways that made him more fully himself.

He is read in schools, adapted for screens, quoted in wilderness cabins and city boardrooms alike. The reason is simple: he is every creature who has ever been asked to survive circumstances beyond its choosing — and has.
